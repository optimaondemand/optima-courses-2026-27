Course Overview

Chemistry I Honors treats chemistry not as a list of facts and formulas but as a centuries-long investigation into the nature of matter, energy, and change. Across nine modules, scholars retrace and extend the same questions that challenged Democritus, Boyle, Lavoisier, Dalton, Mendeleev, the Curies, Rutherford, and Bohr — and connect them to research happening right now. The work is genuinely quantitative: every week scholars do real chemistry, calculating, analyzing data, interpreting models, and working problems, not just reading.

The year moves from what matter is (structure, atoms, the periodic table) to why it changes (bonding and reactions), to how change is measured (stoichiometry, gases, energy, solutions), to how chemistry shapes our world (equilibrium, acids and bases, electrochemistry, and nuclear chemistry). Each module pairs short interactive lessons with hands-on quantitative assignments, an immersive activity on Optima's VR campus, and a cumulative project scholars build and explain in their own words.

What Scholars Will Learn to Do

  • Measure and calculate with precision — SI units, significant figures, and dimensional analysis.
  • Explain the structure of the atom and how the atomic model changed as evidence accumulated.
  • Use the periodic table to predict properties and trends from electron arrangement.
  • Model how atoms bond and predict molecular shape and polarity.
  • Write and balance chemical equations and reason with the mole and conservation of mass.
  • Solve stoichiometry, molarity, gas-law, and calorimetry problems from data.
  • Relate intermolecular forces to the properties of liquids, solids, and solutions.
  • Apply equilibrium, Le Châtelier's principle, and pH to real chemical systems.
  • Analyze redox, electrochemical cells, reaction rates, and nuclear change.
  • Argue from evidence — writing clear Claim–Evidence–Reasoning responses and lab reports.

How This Course Is Delivered

This course is offered in two formats that share the same content, assignments, and expectations: an On-Demand (asynchronous) section you pace yourself, and a Live (synchronous) section that meets on a schedule on Optima's VR campus. Find the one that matches your enrollment.

On-Demand (Asynchronous) Learning

You move through the course at your own pace within each quarter, guided by due dates that keep you on track. All assignments for a quarter open at its start and close at its end. Because the course is On-Demand, there is no grade penalty for missing an individual due date — but any assignment not completed by the end of the quarter receives a zero, and assignments submitted in the final 14 days of a quarter receive a 10% deduction, so steady pacing matters.

Attendance is measured by assignment completion. Per school policy, a scholar who completes no assignment for ten consecutive school days, without notifying the school in advance, may be dropped from the course. Completion means a sincere attempt — real effort on real work. Rushed work, wrong or corrupted files, nonsense submissions, AI-generated filler, and word-salad responses do not count.

Live (Synchronous) Learning

You join scheduled class meetings on Optima's VR campus, where your teacher leads instruction in real time and you learn alongside classmates. The readings, assignments, and projects are the same as the On-Demand course; what differs is the pacing — a Live section moves through the material together on the class schedule, and class time is also used for discussion, lab demonstrations, and guided problem-solving.

Attendance is measured by presence and participation. You are expected to attend the scheduled sessions, on time and ready to take part. If you must miss a class, notify the school in advance; your teacher will tell you how to make up the work.

Appropriate Use of AI

Optima scholars are preparing for a world shaped by artificial intelligence, and learning to use these tools with good judgment is part of that preparation. In chemistry, we treat AI as an instrument, not an oracle: you may use AI tools to explain a concept a different way, check a calculation, or brainstorm — unless a particular assignment says otherwise — but you must always verify what it tells you against real evidence and your own worked math. What AI may never do is the thinking or the work an assignment asks of you: a CER response, a lab report, a self-assessment, or a scientific argument must be genuinely your own. Submitting AI-generated text as your own work is dishonest and, as noted above, counts as an insincere attempt. When you do use an AI tool on an assignment, say so briefly. Above all, build the underlying skill yourself — the goal is to be able to judge whether an answer is any good, which you can only do if you can do the work without it.

Learning in Virtual Reality

Optima is a virtual-reality school. Alongside your Canvas coursework, you will join immersive sessions on Optima's VR campus, where you build atoms and molecules, run reactions, set up gas-law and calorimetry apparatus, and explore chemistry at a scale you could never see in an ordinary classroom. Each module you advance a cumulative VR build and, at the end, record yourself explaining the chemistry it shows. Keep your headset charged and ready, and treat the VR campus with the same courtesy and seriousness you would a physical lab. Every VR activity has a non-VR alternate that meets the same objectives — if you ever cannot get into VR, tell your facilitator so you do not fall behind.

Being an Optima Scholar

Optima's mission is an excellent, virtue-centered education, and virtue is at the center of this course as much as any other. Every scholar practices the seven virtues that shape life at Optima — courage, courtesy, honesty, perseverance, self-government, service, and responsibility. Chemistry gives each a home: wonder at the hidden order of matter, perseverance through models refined over centuries, self-government in the discipline of precise measurement, and responsibility for chemistry's power in the world — with honesty, letting evidence outweigh preference, running through all of it. In practice: do your own honest work; report data as you actually measured it; treat everyone you meet in writing, in discussion, and in VR with respect; come to office hours; and take responsibility for your own pacing and progress. You belong to a house and a community of scholars, not only to a course.
